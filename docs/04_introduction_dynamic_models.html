Example 4.1: Competing hardwood and softwood trees

Source: textbook PDF pp. 122–126.

Problem statement

Model two populations of trees, hardwood and softwood, in an unmanaged forest. Each grows logistically in the absence of the other. Let be intrinsic growth rates, self-limitation coefficients and competition coefficients. Use

Find every equilibrium in the nonnegative quadrant, including the boundaries. Determine the algebraic conditions for a physically meaningful coexistence equilibrium. There are no numerical parameter values in this general formulation; do not invent a unique numerical population prediction.

Approach and verification

Factor each population equation before cancelling population variables. Cancelling too early loses the extinction and single-species equilibria. The remaining two linear equations give an interior candidate, but it is biologically feasible only when both populations are nonnegative.

Python · cell 4 · execution 2
H, S = sp.symbols('H S', real=True)

r1, r2, a1, a2, b1, b2 = sp.symbols('r_1 r_2 a_1 a_2 b_1 b_2', positive=True)

f = sp.Matrix([r1 * H - a1 * H ** 2 - b1 * H * S, r2 * S - a2 * S ** 2 - b2 * H * S])

interior = {H: (r1 * a2 - b1 * r2) / (a1 * a2 - b1 * b2), S: (a1 * r2 - b2 * r1) / (a1 * a2 - b1 * b2)}

points = [{H: 0, S: 0}, {H: r1 / a1, S: 0}, {H: 0, S: r2 / a2}, interior]

for point in points:
    assert sp.simplify(f.subs(point)) == sp.zeros(2, 1)

display(points)

print('The interior formula requires a nonzero denominator and two positive coordinates.')

print('At determinant zero, solve the two balance equations again: there may be no interior solution or a continuum.')

Interpretation and limits

An equilibrium is a state with zero rate of change. It is not necessarily attractive. The boundary equilibria and the interior equilibrium all need separate feasibility and stability analysis; a formula with a negative population is not a coexistence prediction.

Example 4.2: Recovery and competition of whale populations

Source: textbook PDF pp. 127–131.

Problem statement

Blue and fin whale populations start at 5,000 and 70,000. Their intrinsic annual growth rates are 0.05 and 0.08, carrying capacities are 150,000 and 400,000, and both lose individuals/year through the modeled competition effect. Use per whale-year as a baseline. Determine equilibria, nullclines and trajectories, and explain whether both species can coexist without harvesting.

The populations are continuous approximations. The model describes competition, not direct transfers between species.

Approach and verification

Use logistic self-limitation plus cross-species competition. Solve the nullcline equations for the positive equilibrium, then integrate from the stated initial populations. Check the equilibrium residual and compare long-time populations with that equilibrium.

from modeling.dynamics import competition_rhs, solve_ivp

assert np.linalg.norm(rhs(0, eq)) < 1e-09

assert rhs(0, [0, 70000])[0] == 0 and rhs(0, [5000, 0])[1] == 0

Interpretation and limits

At competition coefficient 1e-7 the coexistence populations are approximately 35,294 blue whales and 382,353 fin whales. A finite-time result is not an exact equilibrium. Recovery estimates depend strongly on the tolerance, initial state and assumed parameters; this historical toy model is not a current population forecast.

Example 4.3: Manual docking with delayed control

Source: textbook PDF pp. 132–138.

Problem statement

A spacecraft has initial closing velocity 50 m/s. At observation , the operator chooses acceleration , taking seconds to implement it, then waits seconds before observing again. During the adjustment the previous acceleration remains active. Use gain and specify for this two-velocity initialization.

Derive and simulate . The state must include the delay; treating alone as the complete state loses information. Determine whether closing velocity tends to zero.

Approach and verification

A velocity-only description is not a complete state when the previous control action continues during the next adjustment interval. Add the previous velocity, producing a two-component first-order map. Compare repeated iteration with a matrix power.

Python · cell 14 · execution 6
from modeling.dynamics import docking_matrix, iterate_linear

M = docking_matrix(0.02)

initial = np.array([50.0, 50.0])

states = iterate_linear(M, initial, 40)

assert np.allclose(states[1], M @ initial)

assert np.allclose(states[-1], np.linalg.matrix_power(M, 40) @ initial)

assert np.linalg.norm(states[-1]) < 0.001

Interpretation and limits

The second state coordinate stores memory rather than another physical object. A recurrence with a delay can become unstable even when its corresponding instantaneous-feedback model is stable. State choice is part of modeling, not merely notation.

Example 4.4: A discrete vector field versus continuous decay

Source: textbook PDF pp. 135–136.

Problem statement

For the two-dimensional state , compare the difference equation with the differential equation . Start at . Show the behavior at and derive the exact stability condition for the discrete model.

Approach and verification

For the two-dimensional linear map, each coordinate is multiplied by the same factor at each step. Its magnitude determines contraction or growth, and its sign determines whether the direction alternates. Compare the recurrence with its closed-form solution.

# Keep bounded solutions on a readable scale, rather than hiding them behind divergence.
for lam in [0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.2]:
    result = iterate_map(lambda n, x: (1 - lam) * x, [1.0, 0.6], 25)

Interpretation and limits

Stability uses the multiplier magnitude, not its sign alone. A negative multiplier with magnitude less than one gives oscillatory convergence. Boundary values require separate analysis: a two-cycle is not convergence to the equilibrium.
