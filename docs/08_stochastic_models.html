Example 8.1: Aquarium inventory and lost sales

Source: textbook PDF pp. 254–264.

Problem statement

A pet store begins each week with 1, 2 or 3 aquariums. Weekly demand is Poisson with mean 1, independent between weeks. After the week's sales, the store replenishes to 3 only when inventory reaches zero; otherwise it places no order. Delivery is assumed complete before the next week. Unfilled demand is lost, not backlogged.

Construct the transition matrix for beginning-of-week inventory, compute its stationary distribution, and estimate the long-run probability of at least one lost sale. Compare this with the probability of finishing a week at zero stock: demand equal to stock causes a stockout but not a lost sale.

Approach and verification

Choose the beginning-of-week inventory as the state and enumerate every possible demand outcome, including all demands exceeding stock. Excess demand is lost rather than carried forward. Solve the stationary balance equations and check row sums and residuals.

Example 8.2: A three-state discrete Markov chain

Source: textbook PDF pp. 255–259.

Problem statement

Let a row distribution evolve as , with

Starting in state 1, find the stationary distribution and compare repeated multiplication with direct solution of , . Explain why a stationary distribution and convergence to it are different concepts.

Approach and verification

Use a row-vector convention consistently: distributions multiply P on the right. Solve stationary balance together with normalization, then compare with repeated distribution updates. A unique stationary vector does not always imply ordinary convergence from every initial distribution.

Interpretation and limits

Periodic chains can oscillate forever while still having a unique stationary distribution. Testing stationarity and testing convergence are separate tasks. The reusable solver rejects nonunique stationary distributions rather than returning an arbitrary one.

Example 8.3: A finite-capacity forklift repair queue

Source: textbook PDF pp. 264–274.

Problem statement

Forklifts arrive at mean rate per month. A single mechanic has exponential service times at rate per month (the book's rounded value of working-month repairs). Space is limited to 27 forklifts, including the one in service. For the finite M/M/1/27 model, arrivals when full are blocked.

Find the probability that the mechanic is busy, the expected number in the system and the blocking probability. Then use the admitted arrival rate, not the offered rate, in Little's law to estimate mean time in the system. Compare the rounded and unrounded service rate as a sensitivity check.

Approach and verification

Construct a finite birth–death process with arrivals when space remains and service when at least one vehicle is present. The stationary probabilities are normalized geometric weights. Compute them in log space to avoid overflow for extreme rate ratios.

Example 8.4: Embedded jump chains versus time occupancy

Source: textbook PDF pp. 265–269.

Problem statement

A continuous-time process jumps according to

and its exponential holding times have means 1, 2 and 3 time units in states 1, 2 and 3. Compute both the long-run fraction of jumps landing in each state and the long-run fraction of time spent there. Construct the generator and verify its stationary distribution independently.

Approach and verification

A transition matrix gives probabilities per discrete step; a generator gives rates per unit time. Build the generator, solve stationary balance and verify it independently using the transition matrix obtained by exponentiation.

Python · cell 19 · execution 8
from modeling.probability import stationary_distribution

from scipy.linalg import expm

P = np.array([[0, 1 / 3, 2 / 3], [0.5, 0, 0.5], [0.75, 0.25, 0]])

holding = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0])

jump = stationary_distribution(P)

time = jump * holding

time /= time.sum()

Q = P / holding[:, None]

np.fill_diagonal(Q, -1 / holding)

assert np.allclose(time, stationary_distribution(Q, continuous=True))

assert np.allclose(time, [7 / 35, 8 / 35, 20 / 35])

assert np.allclose(expm(100 * Q)[0], time, atol=1e-09)

display(pd.DataFrame({'state': [1, 2, 3], 'jump fraction': jump, 'holding mean': holding, 'time fraction': time}))

Interpretation and limits

Stationary balance for continuous time is pi Q = 0, whereas for discrete time it is pi P = pi. Off-diagonal generator entries are nonnegative but diagonal entries are not probabilities. Confusing rates with probabilities can produce a plausible-looking but invalid model.

Example 8.5: Historical mortgage-index regression

Source: textbook PDF pp. 274–283.

Problem statement

Use the 37 monthly CM1 observations from June 1986 through June 1989 in data/cm1.csv, transcribed from Table 8.1. Let denote June 1986 and fit by least squares. Predict the index for May 1990 ().

Report the fitted coefficients, residual diagnostics, and both a confidence interval for the conditional mean and a prediction interval for a future observation. Those intervals are different. This is a retrospective textbook exercise, not a current financial prediction. The usual OLS intervals assume the residual model is correct; inspect autocorrelation before trusting them.

Approach and verification

Fit an intercept and a time slope using least squares. Check against a direct linear-algebra implementation, then distinguish an interval for the mean response from an interval for a future observation. Inspect residuals before interpreting the latter.

Example 8.6: Autoregression and accumulated forecast uncertainty

Source: textbook PDF pp. 283–292.

Problem statement

Use the same CM1 dataset, now fitting from through 37. Predict recursively through . Compare residual standard deviation and autocorrelation with the trend-only fit.

Calculate horizon-dependent conditional forecast variance, assuming independent innovations with variance and treating fitted coefficients as fixed:

The original notebook used a one-step interval at an 11-month horizon. That misses accumulated future innovations. Even the corrected conditional interval below does not include parameter-estimation uncertainty or future model changes.

Approach and verification

Add the preceding observed value to the regression design. Future values are unavailable, so multi-step forecasts must be iterated using previous forecasts. Innovation variance follows V(h) = sigma² + phi² V(h−1), not a constant one-step variance.

Python · cell 29 · execution 12
# Independently recurse the mean without relying on a pre-entered textbook answer.

from modeling.regression import ar1_forecast

ar_data = pd.DataFrame({'y': cm1.cm1.iloc[1:].to_numpy(), 't': cm1.t.iloc[1:].to_numpy(), 'lag1': cm1.cm1.iloc[:-1].to_numpy()})

fit = sm.OLS(ar_data.y, sm.add_constant(ar_data[['t', 'lag1']])).fit()

forecast = ar1_forecast(fit.params['const'], fit.params['t'], fit.params['lag1'], cm1.cm1.iloc[-1], 38, 11, np.sqrt(fit.scale))

assert np.isclose(forecast.innovation_variance.iloc[-1], fit.scale * np.sum(fit.params['lag1'] ** (2 * np.arange(11))))

assert np.all(np.diff(forecast.innovation_variance) >= 0)

value = float(cm1.cm1.iloc[-1])

for time in range(38, 49):
    value = fit.params['const'] + fit.params['t'] * time + fit.params['lag1'] * value

assert np.isclose(value, forecast.forecast.iloc[-1])

print(fit.summary())

display(forecast)

print('The interval conditions on fitted coefficients; parameter uncertainty is not included.')

Interpretation and limits

The displayed multi-step interval conditions on estimated coefficients and therefore omits parameter uncertainty. A high in-sample fit is not enough to choose a forecasting model. Residual dependence and genuinely withheld observations provide different checks.
